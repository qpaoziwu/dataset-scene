Experimental feature


Recreating a day of light from real data


The synthetic skybox rebuilds outdoor light for a real place and a real moment. Step the clock and the same scene renders as a timeline, sunrise to sunset on a chosen date, with every frame's light traceable to a published model or a measurement. Open Sun & sky beside the viewer and press Play the day to watch it.


How one frame's light is built


    

  1. Where is the sun? Latitude, longitude, date, local time and time zone go through NOAA's solar equations. Daylight saving comes from the time zone database. Checked against NREL's reference algorithm: within 0.013° of elevation, sunrise and sunset within seconds.
    2. 

  2. How strong is the light? Open-Meteo's hourly direct and diffuse sunlight for that spot sets it: the ERA5 reanalysis for past dates, forecast models for recent days and the next two weeks. Values are interpolated to the minute, and CAMS aerosol levels set the haze. Where no data exists (beyond the forecast range), the Ineichen–Perez clear-sky model fills in from air mass, altitude and haze.
    3. 

  3. What does the sky look like? A clear sky follows the CIE standard clear-sky shape. With weather data, the split between direct and diffuse light picks one of eight Perez all-weather sky types, from clear to overcast. The colours of sun and sky come from NREL's SPCTRL2 spectral model, so a low sun turns warm.
    4. 

  4. Bake it. The sky becomes a 512 × 256 HDR map, scaled so it delivers exactly the measured diffuse light onto the table. The sun becomes a directional light carrying the direct light. Both renderers read the same file. Exposure works like a camera's auto exposure at ISO 100: it meters the light on the table but stops at EV 1, so daylight looks normal and moonlight comes out dark, as it should.
    5. 

  5. After sunset, the Moon. The Moon's position (a lunar theory with its main perturbations and topocentric parallax, within 0.08° of astropy) and its phase set the moonlight. Its brightness and the glow it scatters across the sky follow the Krisciunas & Schaefer 1991 moonlight model, on top of a natural dark sky of 21.6 mag/arcsec², with the same haze as the daytime model.
    6. 

  6. Step the clock. Each time step gives a new sun and sky and a new frame. The light parameters (sun angles, W/m², sky model, data source) are saved in every view's JSON next to the camera, so each image says what light it was made under.
    7. 



What a timeline gives a dataset


    

  • The same geometry under controlled, realistic light. Depth, normals and the semantic and instance maps stay identical, so only the light changes.
    • 

  • Splits by condition: train on clear mornings, test on overcast afternoons, and see exactly which light a model fails under.
    • 

  • Reproducible from a date and coordinates. Anyone can regenerate the frame, or compare it with a photo taken at that place and time.
    • 



Limits, stated plainly


    

  • Weather data comes as hourly means, so fast changes such as a cloud passing over the sun are smoothed out.
    • 

  • Cloud amount is real, cloud positions are not: there are no cloud shapes or moving cloud shadows.
    • 

  • No twilight, clouds at night or city light: after sunset the light comes only from the Moon and a natural dark sky, as if far from any town.
    • 

  • No ground: nothing below the horizon reflects light, so only the sky and the sun light the scene. The backdrop below the horizon is a gradient from the horizon colour, for looks only; it adds no light. A cloudy sky's colour is blended toward neutral rather than modelled.

    The objects and their fine detail maps are generated in the browser. Tabletop textures are Poly Haven scans; the phones are a supplied glTF model with its own materials. Boxes are computed from each object's 3D box corners, so they run slightly looser than mask-based COCO boxes. The path tracer ignores displacement and screen-space effects; it computes shadows, occlusion and reflections directly. In Sun & sky mode the sun's direction, strength and colour and the sky's brightness pattern come from published solar and daylight models, or from measured hourly weather, and after sunset from the Moon's position, phase and a moonlight model; ratios between sun, sky and ground are physical and the exposure is metered.
